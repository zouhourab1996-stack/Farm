Jessour in Tunisia: Traditional Water Harvesting in Dry Landscapes

In parts of arid southern Tunisia, traditional jessour systems use small barriers and terraced areas across gullies and wadi channels to slow runoff and retain water and sediment for cultivation.

How the system works

Jessour structures are built to intercept seasonal runoff and sediment. By slowing water and allowing some of it to collect behind barriers, they can help sustain cultivation in difficult environments. Their design is connected to local topography, hydrology and accumulated community knowledge.

Why jessour matter for biodiversity

Research on the jessour landscape around Douiret describes how water-harvesting structures can make cultivation possible and support a range of cultivated plants and associated flora and fauna. These traditional systems are both cultural landscapes and practical responses to water scarcity.

Care, maintenance and local knowledge

Traditional water structures require maintenance and a clear understanding of local runoff patterns. They should not be copied without studying the catchment and site conditions. The most useful lesson is the relationship between people, landform, water and long-term stewardship.
