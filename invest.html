The one-paragraph answer

This is a real asset, not a paper one: 16 hectares of rural Tunisian farmland, owned by the founder, carrying a young olive grove and — the decisive part — a water source on the land itself. Tunisia farms 1.9 million hectares of olives, but only about one fifth of that area is irrigated, and that fifth produces a wildly disproportionate share of the crop. Water is the moat. We are looking for investors and partners to accelerate the next phases of a plan that is already moving, documented in public, and small enough to actually understand.

Why water changes everything here

The olive tree's fame for surviving drought is deserved — but surviving is not producing. Young trees need steady water through their first summers to establish, and a grove that irrigates through dry spells bears earlier and heavier than one that waits for rain. Around 84% of Tunisia's cultivated land is already olive country, yet only roughly 20% of the olive area is irrigated — which is exactly why irrigated parcels punch far above their weight. Field reporting from Tunisian farming regions keeps repeating one line: serious money moves to where the groundwater is. The European Bank for Reconstruction and Development has put €49 million into irrigation access for thousands of Tunisian farmers — the direction of the whole sector is water, water, water.

That is the entire investment case of this parcel in one sentence: the land already has the thing everyone else is digging for.
