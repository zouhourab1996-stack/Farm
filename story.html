Tunisia Just Became the World's Second-Largest Olive Oil Producer. Its Next Grove Is Where You Come In.

By Anis Touati · October 5, 2026 · 5 min read

Here are the numbers, straight up: in the 2025/26 season Tunisia harvested a record 450,000–500,000 tonnes of olive oil (the International Olive Council reports ~500,000; USDA estimates 470,000), overtaking Turkey, Italy and Greece to become the world's second-largest producer, behind only Spain. It's the biggest season in the country's history — and the quiet problem underneath the headline is that the trees themselves are getting old.

What the record season actually says

Tunisia's crop jumped about 38% year-on-year — from roughly 340,000 tonnes in 2024/25 to 470,000 by USDA's count — while Turkey's nearly halved, reshuffling the world ranking almost overnight. Exports climbed in step, to around 400,000 tonnes. According to the IOC, the olive sector now accounts for more than half of Tunisia's agricultural exports, and remarkably, about 80% of national production is organic — largely because smallholders never had the money for chemicals in the first place.

The catch behind the celebration

Two things temper the record. First, olive trees are alternate bearers — a bumper year is often followed by a lean one, so single-season rankings flatter. Second, and more important: much of Tunisia's grove is aging. Many productive trees today were planted by grandparents. Replacing them is a 10-to-15-year bet: a seedling yields its first real harvest after roughly 5–7 years (full production takes longer), and nobody pays a farmer's bills in year six of waiting.

That's the structural gap — and it's exactly the kind of long-horizon, low-glamour investment that markets skip and communities have to fund themselves. One tree at a time is slow. It's also the only speed that has ever worked for olives.

What one tree actually does

Beyond the economics, the olive tree is a quietly extraordinary plant: drought-resistant, soil-holding, carbon-sequestering, and famous for outliving its planters by centuries. In a country where water is the binding constraint of the century, planting the tree that asks for the least of it is close to a strategic act. Frequently asked questions

Is Tunisia really the world's second-largest olive oil producer?

Yes — for the 2025/26 season, per the International Olive Council: a record ~500,000 tonnes, behind only Spain and ahead of Turkey, Italy and Greece. USDA's estimate is 470,000 tonnes.

How much olive oil does Tunisia produce per year?

It swings with the trees' natural alternate-bearing cycle and rainfall: ~340,000 t in 2024/25, then a record 450,000–500,000 t in 2025/26 depending on the source.

Why does the ranking matter to small farmers?

The record boosted exports, but the national grove is aging and replanting is a 10–15 year investment smallholders can rarely fund alone. That's the gap tree adoption exists to fill.
